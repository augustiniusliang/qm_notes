\section{Lie Groups and Lie Algebras}
\label{sec:appendix-lie-groups}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Definition (Lie group).} A \textbf{Lie group} $G$ is a group that is also a smooth manifold, such that the group operations (multiplication $G \times G \to G$ and inversion $G \to G$) are smooth maps. The dimension of the manifold is the \textbf{dimension} of the group.

The main text's central examples are the Galilei group (10 parameters: 3 rotations, 3 boosts, 3 translations, 1 time translation) and the rotation group SO(3) (3 parameters: the Euler angles). For matrix groups --- closed subgroups of $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{C})$ --- smoothness is automatic from the matrix entries, and the dimension is the number of independent real parameters. SO(3) is the group of real $3 \times 3$ orthogonal matrices with determinant $+1$; SU(2) is the group of complex $2 \times 2$ unitary matrices with determinant $+1$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Definition (Lie algebra).} The \textbf{Lie algebra} $\mathfrak{g}$ of a Lie group $G$ is the tangent space to $G$ at the identity element, equipped with the Lie bracket $[X, Y]$ derived from the group commutator. Concretely, for a matrix group, $\mathfrak{g}$ is the vector space of matrices $X$ such that $e^{tX} \in G$ for all real $t$, and the Lie bracket is the matrix commutator $[X, Y] = XY - YX$.

The Lie algebra is a vector space of the same (real) dimension as the group. It captures the group's structure infinitesimally: every element of $G$ in the connected component of the identity can be written as $e^X$ for some $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, and the group multiplication law is encoded in the bracket through the Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff (BCH) formula:
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        e^X e^Y = e^{X + Y + \frac{1}{2}[X,Y] + \frac{1}{12}[X,[X,Y]] - \frac{1}{12}[Y,[X,Y]] + \cdots}.
        \label{eq:bch-formula}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
Chapter~4's~\eqref{eq:lie-bracket} is the leading nontrivial term of this expansion.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Definition (Exponential map).} The \textbf{exponential map} $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ sends $X \mapsto e^X$. For a matrix group, $e^X$ is the ordinary matrix exponential $\sum_{n=0}^\infty X^n/n!$. The exponential map is a local diffeomorphism near the identity: every group element sufficiently close to $e$ is $e^X$ for a unique small $X \in \mathfrak{g}$. Globally, the exponential map is surjective onto the connected component of the identity for compact groups (like SU(2)) but not in general (it fails for $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})$, an instance the main text does not need).

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Structure constants.} Choose a basis $\{T_a\}_{a=1}^{\dim \mathfrak{g}}$ for the Lie algebra. The Lie bracket of any two basis elements is again in the algebra:
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        [T_a, T_b] = \sum_{c} f_{ab}{}^c\,T_c,
        \label{eq:app-structure-constants}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
where the real numbers $f_{ab}{}^c$ are the \textbf{structure constants} of the algebra. They are antisymmetric in the first two indices ($f_{ab}{}^c = -f_{ba}{}^c$) and satisfy the Jacobi identity:
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        \sum_{d} \bigl( f_{ab}{}^d f_{dc}{}^e + f_{bc}{}^d f_{da}{}^e + f_{ca}{}^d f_{db}{}^e \bigr) = 0,
        \label{eq:jacobi-identity}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
which is the algebraic shadow of the associativity of group multiplication.

For the rotation group SO(3), the Lie algebra is $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ with basis $\{J_1, J_2, J_3\}$ and structure constants $[J_i, J_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$. The factor $i\hbar$ in the quantum-mechanical convention reflects that the physical generators are self-adjoint operators (observables) representing the abstract algebra elements; the abstract algebra has structure constants $\epsilon_{ijk}$ without the $i\hbar$. The main text's~\eqref{eq:galilei-algebra} gives the complete Galilean algebra in the quantum convention. The distinction between the abstract algebra and its quantum representative is the content of representation theory.

\section{Representations}
\label{sec:appendix-representations}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Definition (Representation).} A \textbf{representation} of a Lie group $G$ on a vector space $V$ is a homomorphism $\Pi : G \to \mathrm{GL}(V)$: a smooth map such that $\Pi(g_1 g_2) = \Pi(g_1)\Pi(g_2)$ and $\Pi(e) = I$. The dimension of the representation is $\dim V$. A representation of a Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ on $V$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism $\pi : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(V)$: a linear map such that $\pi([X,Y]) = [\pi(X), \pi(Y)]$.

For a matrix group, the defining representation is the obvious one: $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ acts on $\mathbb{R}^3$ by matrix multiplication, $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ acts on $\mathbb{C}^2$. The quantum-mechanical state space carries a \textbf{unitary} representation: $V = \mathcal{H}$ and each $\Pi(g)$ is unitary on $\mathcal{H}$. By Stone's theorem (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-stone}), the one-parameter subgroups $t \mapsto \Pi(e^{tX})$ are generated by self-adjoint operators $\pi(X)$, and the Lie algebra representation $\pi$ produces the physical observables: momentum, angular momentum, energy.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Definition (Irreducible representation).} A representation is \textbf{irreducible} if $V$ contains no nontrivial invariant subspace: the only subspaces $W \subseteq V$ such that $\Pi(g)W \subseteq W$ for all $g \in G$ are $\{0\}$ and $V$. Irreducible representations are abbreviated \textbf{irreps}.

The irreps of a group are its elementary building blocks: every finite-dimensional unitary representation decomposes uniquely as a direct sum of irreps (complete reducibility, which holds for compact groups). The physical states of an elementary system --- a particle of definite mass and spin --- transform in an irrep of the symmetry group. The $(2j+1)$-dimensional multiplets of Chapter~6 are the irreps of SU(2).

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Lemma (Schur's Lemma).} \emph{Let $\Pi_1$ and $\Pi_2$ be two irreducible representations of $G$ on vector spaces $V_1$ and $V_2$, and let $T : V_1 \to V_2$ be a linear map that intertwines the representations: $T \circ \Pi_1(g) = \Pi_2(g) \circ T$ for all $g \in G$. Then either $T = 0$, or $T$ is an isomorphism (so $\Pi_1$ and $\Pi_2$ are equivalent). In particular, if $\Pi_1 = \Pi_2$ is a finite-dimensional complex irrep, then $T = \lambda I$ for some scalar $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$.}

\emph{Proof sketch.} The kernel $\ker T$ is an invariant subspace of $V_1$: if $Tv = 0$, then $T(\Pi_1(g)v) = \Pi_2(g)(Tv) = 0$. By irreducibility, $\ker T$ is either $\{0\}$ or $V_1$. Similarly, the image $\operatorname{im} T$ is invariant in $V_2$. If $T \neq 0$, then $\ker T = \{0\}$ (injective) and $\operatorname{im} T = V_2$ (surjective), so $T$ is an isomorphism. For the second statement, let $\lambda$ be any eigenvalue of $T$ (which exists because $V_1$ is a finite-dimensional complex vector space). Then $T - \lambda I$ also intertwines the representation and has a nontrivial kernel, so by the first part, $T - \lambda I = 0$. \hfill $\blacksquare$

Schur's lemma is the engine of the Wigner--Eckart theorem (Chapter~6, §6.7): the reduced matrix element is the scalar $\lambda$ that survives after the geometric (representation-theoretic) part is extracted. The orthogonality relations for matrix elements (the great orthogonality theorem below) are its global corollary.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Theorem (Great Orthogonality Theorem for compact groups; stated without proof).} \emph{Let $G$ be a compact Lie group with normalized Haar measure $\dd g$. Let $\Pi^{(\alpha)}$ and $\Pi^{(\beta)}$ be inequivalent irreducible unitary representations of $G$ with dimensions $d_\alpha$ and $d_\beta$. Then the matrix elements satisfy}
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        \int_G \Pi^{(\alpha)}_{ij}(g)^*\,\Pi^{(\beta)}_{kl}(g)\,\dd g = \frac{1}{d_\alpha}\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,\delta_{ik}\,\delta_{jl}.
        \label{eq:great-orthogonality}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}

The proof is collected in Hall, Chapter~5. For the rotation group SO(3) (and its cover SU(2)), the Haar measure is $\dd g = (1/8\pi^2)\sin\beta\,\dd\alpha\,\dd\beta\,\dd\gamma$ in Euler angles, with $\alpha \in [0,2\pi)$, $\beta \in [0,\pi]$, $\gamma \in [0,2\pi)$ (doubled for SU(2)), and the theorem yields the orthogonality of the Wigner D-matrices stated in the next section.

\section{SU(2) Representation Theory}
\label{sec:appendix-su2-reps}

The group SU(2) is the universal covering group of SO(3). Its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ is the real vector space of traceless anti-Hermitian $2 \times 2$ matrices. The conventional physics basis uses the self-adjoint generators $J_k = (i\hbar/2)\sigma_k$, satisfying $[J_i, J_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$, whose complexified algebra is $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C}) \cong \mathfrak{so}(3)_{\mathbb{C}}$. Every finite-dimensional irreducible representation is obtained from the highest-weight construction.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Theorem (Classification of SU(2) irreps; proof sketched in Chapter~6).} \emph{For every $j = 0, \tfrac12, 1, \tfrac32, \ldots$, there exists exactly one (up to equivalence) irreducible unitary representation of SU(2) of dimension $2j+1$, denoted $D^{(j)}$. In an orthonormal basis $\{\ket{j,m}\}_{m=-j}^j$, the generators act as:}
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{align}
        J_z \ket{j,m} &= \hbar m \ket{j,m}, \label{eq:su2-jz} \\
        J_\pm \ket{j,m} &= \hbar\sqrt{j(j+1) - m(m \pm 1)}\,\ket{j,m \pm 1}, \label{eq:su2-jpm}
    \end{align}
\end{linenomath}
\emph{with $J_\pm = J_x \pm iJ_y$. The Casimir invariant is $J^2 \ket{j,m} = \hbar^2 j(j+1) \ket{j,m}$.}

The construction proceeds by the method of Chapter~6, §6.2: the ladder operator algebra $[J_z, J_\pm] = \pm\hbar J_\pm$, $[J_+, J_-] = 2\hbar J_z$ forces the spectrum. The highest-weight state $\ket{j,j}$ is defined by $J_+ \ket{j,j} = 0$, and all other states are obtained by repeated action of $J_-$. The normalization~\eqref{eq:su2-jpm} follows from $\|J_-\ket{j,m}\|^2 = \hbar^2[j(j+1) - m(m-1)]$. The condition that norms be nonnegative forces $m$ to lie between $-j$ and $+j$, and since $m$ changes by integer steps, $2j$ must be a nonnegative integer.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Definition (Character).} The \textbf{character} of a representation $D$ is the trace $\chi^{(j)}(g) := \tr D^{(j)}(g)$. For SU(2), every group element is conjugate to a rotation $U_{\bm n}(\theta) = e^{-i\theta J_n/\hbar}$ about some axis $\bm n$, so
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        \chi^{(j)}(\theta) = \sum_{m=-j}^j e^{-im\theta} = \frac{\sin\bigl[(2j+1)\theta/2\bigr]}{\sin(\theta/2)},
        \label{eq:su2-character}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
where $\theta$ is the rotation angle (the class parameter). The character determines the representation uniquely: two representations are equivalent if and only if their characters coincide. The dimension is $\chi^{(j)}(0) = 2j+1$. The orthogonality of characters,
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} \chi^{(j)}(\theta)^*\,\chi^{(j')}(\theta)\,\sin^2(\theta/2)\,\dd\theta = \delta_{jj'},
        \label{eq:character-orthogonality}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
is the class-function reduction of the great orthogonality theorem.

\section{Wigner D-Matrices}
\label{sec:appendix-d-matrices}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Definition (Wigner D-matrix).} The $(2j+1) \times (2j+1)$ matrix elements
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        D^{(j)}_{m'm}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma) := \braket{j,m'|\,e^{-i\alpha J_z/\hbar}\,e^{-i\beta J_y/\hbar}\,e^{-i\gamma J_z/\hbar}\,|j,m},
        \label{eq:D-matrix-def-app}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
are the \textbf{Wigner D-matrices}, the matrix representatives of the Euler-angle parametrization in the irrep $j$. They factor as $D^{(j)}_{m'm} = e^{-i\alpha m'} d^{(j)}_{m'm}(\beta)\,e^{-i\gamma m}$, where
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        d^{(j)}_{m'm}(\beta) := \braket{j,m'|e^{-i\beta J_y/\hbar}|j,m}
        \label{eq:small-d}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
is Wigner's (small) $d$-matrix, real-valued under the Condon--Shortley phase convention. Explicit formulas exist in terms of Jacobi polynomials, but the recurrence relations derived from the $J_\pm$ algebra are more useful computationally.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Properties.} (i)~\emph{Unitarity:} $\sum_{m'} D^{(j)*}_{m'm} D^{(j)}_{m'm''} = \delta_{mm''}$. (ii)~\emph{Composition:} $D^{(j)}_{m'm}(R_1 R_2) = \sum_{m''} D^{(j)}_{m'm''}(R_1) D^{(j)}_{m''m}(R_2)$. (iii)~\emph{Orthogonality} (great orthogonality theorem specialized to SU(2)):
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        \int_0^{2\pi}\!\frac{\dd\alpha}{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\!\frac{\sin\beta\,\dd\beta}{2}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\frac{\dd\gamma}{2\pi}\; D^{(j)*}_{m'm}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\,D^{(j')}_{\mu'\mu}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma) = \frac{1}{2j+1}\,\delta_{jj'}\,\delta_{m'\mu'}\,\delta_{m\mu}.
        \label{eq:D-orthogonality}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
(iv)~\emph{Connection to spherical harmonics:} For integer $j = \ell$, the D-matrix with $m = 0$ (or $m' = 0$) reduces to the spherical harmonic:
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        D^{(\ell)}_{m0}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma) = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}}\;Y_\ell^{m*}(\beta,\alpha),
        \label{eq:D-to-Y}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
where the Euler angles $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$ correspond to spherical coordinates $(\theta = \beta, \phi = \alpha)$. This is the link between the group-theoretic construction of Chapter~6, §6.1 and the analytic wave functions of §6.4.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{The $j=1/2$ case.} The fundamental representation gives
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        d^{(1/2)}(\beta) = \begin{pmatrix}
            \cos(\beta/2) & -\sin(\beta/2) \\
            \sin(\beta/2) & \cos(\beta/2)
        \end{pmatrix},
        \label{eq:d-half}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
from which the spin-$\bm n$ ONB of Chapter~6~\eqref{eq:spin-n-derived} and the half-angle law~\eqref{eq:half-angle-law} follow directly.

\section{The Clebsch--Gordan Series: Proof Sketch}
\label{sec:appendix-cg}

The Clebsch--Gordan problem is: given two irreps $j_1$ and $j_2$ of SU(2), decompose their tensor product into irreducibles. The answer is the Clebsch--Gordan series.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Theorem (Clebsch--Gordan series).} \emph{The tensor product representation $D^{(j_1)} \otimes D^{(j_2)}$ decomposes as}
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        D^{(j_1)} \otimes D^{(j_2)} \cong \bigoplus_{J = |j_1 - j_2|}^{j_1 + j_2} D^{(J)},
        \label{eq:app-cg-series}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
\emph{where each irrep $J$ appears exactly once. Equivalently, the coupled basis $\{\ket{j_1,j_2;J,M}\}$ and the uncoupled basis $\{\ket{j_1,m_1} \otimes \ket{j_2,m_2}\}$ are related by}
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        \ket{j_1,j_2;J,M} = \sum_{m_1,m_2} \braket{j_1,m_1;j_2,m_2|J,M}\,\ket{j_1,m_1} \otimes \ket{j_2,m_2},
        \label{eq:cg-expansion}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
\emph{where $\braket{j_1,m_1;j_2,m_2|J,M}$ are the Clebsch--Gordan coefficients, real under the Condon--Shortley phase convention.}

\medskip\noindent\textit{Proof sketch.} The proof proceeds by character theory. The character of the tensor product is the product of characters: $\chi^{(j_1 \otimes j_2)}(\theta) = \chi^{(j_1)}(\theta)\,\chi^{(j_2)}(\theta)$. Using the closed form~\eqref{eq:su2-character},
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        \chi^{(j_1)}(\theta)\,\chi^{(j_2)}(\theta)
        = \sum_{m_1=-j_1}^{j_1} e^{-im_1\theta} \sum_{m_2=-j_2}^{j_2} e^{-im_2\theta}
        = \sum_{m_1,m_2} e^{-i(m_1+m_2)\theta}.
        \label{eq:product-character}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
For each integer $M = m_1 + m_2$ (which ranges from $-(j_1+j_2)$ to $j_1+j_2$), the coefficient of $e^{-iM\theta}$ is the number of pairs $(m_1,m_2)$ summing to $M$. This number is a step function: it equals $j_1+j_2 - |M| + 1$ for $|M| \ge |j_1-j_2|$, and $2\min(j_1,j_2)+1$ for $|M| \le |j_1-j_2|$. On the other hand, the character of $\bigoplus_J D^{(J)}$ is $\sum_J \sum_{m=-J}^J e^{-im\theta}$. Matching the multiplicities of each $e^{-iM\theta}$ term on both sides yields that $J$ ranges from $|j_1-j_2|$ to $j_1+j_2$ in integer steps, each exactly once. The coefficients $\braket{j_1,m_1;j_2,m_2|J,M}$ are then the matrix elements of the unitary change-of-basis between the uncoupled and coupled bases; they vanish unless $m_1+m_2 = M$ and $|j_1-j_2| \le J \le j_1+j_2$ (the triangle condition). \hfill $\blacksquare$

Explicit formulas for the CG coefficients exist (the Racah closed form cited in Chapter~6), but for computational purposes the recurrence relations derived from applying $J_\pm = J_{1\pm} + J_{2\pm}$ to both sides of~\eqref{eq:cg-expansion} are more practical. The Condon--Shortley phase convention fixes the sign of the coefficient with the largest $m_1$ to be positive, and all other coefficients follow from the ladder recurrences.

\section{Racah Algebra: 6-$j$ and 9-$j$ Symbols}
\label{sec:appendix-racah}

When three or four angular momenta are coupled, the order of coupling matters: $(j_1 \otimes j_2) \otimes j_3$ and $j_1 \otimes (j_2 \otimes j_3)$ produce different coupled bases, related by a unitary transformation whose matrix elements are the Racah $W$-coefficients or, in their symmetric form, the 6-$j$ symbols.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Definition (6-$j$ symbol).} The \textbf{6-$j$ symbol}
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{Bmatrix}
            j_1 & j_2 & J_{12} \\
            j_3 & J & J_{23}
        \end{Bmatrix}
        \label{eq:6j}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
is the recoupling coefficient connecting the two schemes for coupling three angular momenta to total $J$:
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        \braket{(j_1,(j_2,j_3)J_{23})J,M | ((j_1,j_2)J_{12},j_3)J,M}
        = (-1)^{j_1+j_2+j_3+J}\sqrt{(2J_{12}+1)(2J_{23}+1)}\,
        \begin{Bmatrix}
            j_1 & j_2 & J_{12} \\
            j_3 & J & J_{23}
        \end{Bmatrix}.
        \label{eq:6j-recoupling}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
The 6-$j$ symbol vanishes unless the four triads $(j_1,j_2,J_{12})$, $(j_2,j_3,J_{23})$, $(j_1,J,J_{23})$, and $(J_{12},j_3,J)$ each satisfy the triangle condition. It possesses a high degree of symmetry: it is invariant under any permutation of the columns and under the interchange of any two elements in the top row with the corresponding two in the bottom row.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Definition (9-$j$ symbol).} The \textbf{9-$j$ symbol} arises in the recoupling of four angular momenta, typically when two subsystems each carry two angular momenta and the total is formed by coupling either the subsystem totals or the individual components differently:
\begin{linenomath}
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{Bmatrix}
            j_{11} & j_{12} & J_1 \\
            j_{21} & j_{22} & J_2 \\
            J_1'  & J_2'  & J
        \end{Bmatrix}.
        \label{eq:9j}
    \end{equation}
\end{linenomath}
Like the 6-$j$, the 9-$j$ symbol is a sum of products of 6-$j$ symbols (or equivalently of four CG coefficients). It appears in the evaluation of matrix elements of compound operators in coupled schemes, particularly in nuclear and atomic shell-model calculations. Explicit formulas and tabulations are given in Edmonds; the main text names them without deploying them computationally.

\section{Casimir Operators}
\label{sec:appendix-casimir}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Definition (Casimir operator).} A \textbf{Casimir operator} of a Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is an element of the universal enveloping algebra that commutes with every element of $\mathfrak{g}$. For a semisimple Lie algebra, the number of independent Casimirs equals the rank (the dimension of the maximal abelian subalgebra).

For $\mathfrak{su}(2)$, the rank is one, and the unique quadratic Casimir is $J^2 = J_x^2 + J_y^2 + J_z^2$. In any irrep $j$, Schur's lemma forces $J^2 = \lambda I$ with $\lambda = \hbar^2 j(j+1)$, as Chapter~6 proves. For the Galilei algebra, the central element $M$ (mass) is a Casimir (it commutes with every generator), and the internal energy $E_{\mathrm{int}}$ in~\eqref{eq:free-hamiltonian} is the Casimir of the subalgebra that remains after factoring out the central extension. For the Lorentz group (named only as a forward pointer in this nonrelativistic book), the two Casimirs are $P_\mu P^\mu$ (mass-squared) and $W_\mu W^\mu$ (the square of the Pauli--Lubanski vector, encoding spin).

\section{Covering Groups}
\label{sec:appendix-covering}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{The SO(3)/SU(2) double cover.} The rotation group SO(3) is not simply connected: its fundamental group is $\pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(3)) = \mathbb{Z}_2$. Its universal covering group is SU(2), which is simply connected ($\pi_1(\mathrm{SU}(2)) = \{0\}$). The covering map $\Phi : \mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)$ is the 2:1 homomorphism constructed in Chapter~6~\eqref{eq:su2-so3-homomorphism}: both $U$ and $-U$ in SU(2) map to the same rotation matrix in SO(3). The kernel is $\{\pm I\} \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$, so $\mathrm{SO}(3) \cong \mathrm{SU}(2)/\mathbb{Z}_2$.

This topology has direct physical consequences. Projective representations of SO(3) --- representations up to a phase, the quantum-mechanical case by Wigner's theorem --- correspond to ordinary representations of its universal cover SU(2). The integer-spin irreps ($j = 0,1,2,\ldots$) are also representations of SO(3) itself ($D^{(j)}(-I) = I$ when $2j$ is even); the half-integer irreps ($j = 1/2, 3/2, \ldots$) are genuine projective representations of SO(3) ($D^{(j)}(-I) = -I$). This is the group-theoretic origin of the integer/half-integer dichotomy and the spinor $4\pi$ periodicity: a $2\pi$ rotation in the laboratory (SO(3)) is $-I$ in SU(2), and two full turns are required to return a spinor to itself.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{The Lorentz group (named, not built).} The proper orthochronous Lorentz group $\mathrm{SO}^+(3,1)$ is likewise not simply connected; its universal cover is $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})$, a 2:1 covering. The finite-dimensional irreps of $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ are labeled by a pair $(j_1, j_2)$ of SU(2) spins, with dimension $(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1)$. The $(1/2, 0)$ and $(0, 1/2)$ irreps are the left- and right-handed Weyl spinors; their direct sum $(1/2, 0) \oplus (0, 1/2)$ is the Dirac spinor. This appendix names the structure without developing it, as the book is nonrelativistic; the connection to the Galilean spin of Chapter~6 is that the rotation subgroup of the Lorentz group is (the diagonal SU(2)) and the nonrelativistic spin emerges as the restriction of the Lorentz classification to rotations.
